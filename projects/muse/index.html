Muse

(Mobile UX, App Design, UX Research)

Muse is a mobile companion app for museum visitors, designed to support wayfinding, artwork discovery, accessible learning, and reflective documentation before, during, and after a visit.

Museum visitors often move through complex spaces while trying to navigate, understand artworks, and document what they experience. Muse explores how a mobile app can bring together AR wayfinding, contextual artwork information, accessibility features, and a personal/social journal for reflection.

Year 2025

Role UX Designer / UX Researcher

Tools Figma, Miro

Timeline 3 months

Team Research team of 4

Methods Interviews, affinity mapping, wireframes, usability testing, Figma prototype

Problem

Visitors needed orientation, context, and a way to remember the visit.

Museum visitors often struggle to navigate complex layouts, understand artworks in context, and document their experience in a way they can revisit later.

01Disorienting navigation made it easy to miss galleries or exhibits.

02Artwork information was often shallow, inaccessible, or hard to expand.

03Documentation was fragmented across photos, notes, sketches, and memory.

Solution

A companion app for the full museum visit.

Muse supports the visit through guided wayfinding, artwork lookup, accessible learning, and reflective journaling.

01AR navigation for finding galleries and artworks.

02Artwork descriptions and contextual information.

03QR code and number-based artwork lookup.

04Audio description and colour-blind viewing support.

05A journal for saving, reflecting on, and sharing visits.

Research

Interviews focused on how people move, learn, and remember.

To understand current museum habits and pain points, our team interviewed people who self-identified as art enthusiasts or frequent museum visitors, primarily ages 18 to 30. Sessions were recorded with consent, summarized, and synthesized through affinity mapping in Miro.

01How visitors navigate museums.

02How they learn about artworks.

03How they document visits.

04When they prefer solo versus social experiences.

05What tools they currently rely on.

Key insights

Four patterns shaped the product direction.

01Navigation was the clearest pain point. Participants described museums as maze-like, with unclear signage and limited map access. Some photographed physical maps or asked staff for directions, but still missed exhibits they intended to see.

02Documentation was fragmented but meaningful. All participants took photos during visits, often for memory or sharing. Some also used sketches and notes for academic or creative reuse, but these materials were scattered across camera rolls and apps.

03Artwork information felt shallow. Labels and museum platforms often offered only surface-level context. When visitors wanted more depth, they searched externally or gave up. QR codes were received positively because they let people access information without crowding wall labels.

04Social and solo visits served different needs. Visitors valued shared museum experiences, but solo visits allowed for deeper reflection. The app needed to support both modes without forcing the experience to become purely social.

Design response

The app narrowed around four product directions.

01Wayfinding.

02Artwork lookup.

03Guided learning.

04Reflective journaling.

Prototype flows

Five flows carried the prototype.

01Discover a museum.

02Look up an artwork by QR code or number.

03Navigate to a gallery using AR wayfinding.

04Save and write a journal entry.

05View museum information and purchase tickets.

Testing

Task-based testing clarified the rough edges.

The high-fidelity prototype was tested through task-based sessions covering museum discovery, artwork lookup, AR navigation, journal logging, and ticket purchasing.

01AR arrival confirmation needed to be more visually distinct.

02Journal entries needed clearer confirmation that an artwork was linked.

03Back navigation needed to be more consistent across screens.

Iteration

Small interface changes made the flows easier to trust.

01Increased contrast and affordance of the AR arrival card.

02Added a linked artwork preview before publishing a journal entry.

03Improved navigation consistency across artwork, lookup, and map screens.

Takeaways

Research kept the product from becoming too broad.

01Research gave the project focus. Repeated navigation frustrations across interviews gave confidence that wayfinding should be the core differentiator.

02Fewer flows made the product stronger. The concept became clearer when it focused on navigation, lookup, journaling, and museum information rather than trying to solve every museum pain point.

03Documentation was not only social. Users documented museum visits for memory, inspiration, and personal reflection, so the journal needed to support private and social modes.

Future steps

Accessibility and wayfinding need deeper testing.

Future iterations would focus on deeper usability testing for AR navigation, expanding accessibility features such as colour-blind filters and adjustable text size, and exploring multilingual support for non-native English-speaking visitors.
